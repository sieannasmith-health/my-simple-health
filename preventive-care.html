Preventive Care: Where to Start

Preventive care can help you stay informed about your health, identify certain concerns earlier, and keep up with recommended screenings, vaccines, and routine care.

My Simple Health • Preventive Health • 7 min read

Taking care of your health is not only about responding when something feels wrong.

Preventive care focuses on steps that can help protect health, identify certain conditions earlier, and give you useful information about your individual risk factors.

The challenge is knowing where to begin.

Screening recommendations can vary by age, sex, health history, family history, pregnancy status, medications, lifestyle, and other individual risk factors.

Instead of trying to memorize every recommendation, start by understanding the major pieces of preventive care.

What is preventive care?

Preventive care includes health services intended to help prevent illness or identify health concerns before they become more serious.

Depending on the person, preventive care may include routine visits, vaccinations, screening tests, health measurements, counseling, and discussions about individual risk factors.

SIMPLE TAKEAWAY

Preventive care is not one test or one annual appointment. It is an ongoing process of staying informed about your health and the care that may be appropriate for you.

Start with routine healthcare visits

Routine healthcare visits create an opportunity to review your health history, medications, vaccinations, screening needs, symptoms, and risk factors.

They can also be a useful time to discuss changes in sleep, nutrition, physical activity, mental wellbeing, substance use, sexual health, or other areas that may affect your overall health.

How often you need routine visits depends on your individual health needs and the recommendations of your healthcare professional.

Know that screenings are individualized

Health screenings look for certain diseases or risk factors, sometimes before symptoms appear.

Common preventive screenings may include checks related to blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, cervical cancer, osteoporosis, and other health conditions.

But not everyone needs every screening at the same age or at the same interval.

Recommendations can depend on personal and family history, previous test results, age, sex, and other risk factors.

Questions to ask at your next visit

  • Am I due for any preventive screenings?
  • Have any recommendations changed for me?
  • Are there risk factors I should know about?
  • When should I repeat previous tests?
  • Is there anything I should monitor between visits?

Understand your health numbers

Certain health measurements can provide useful information about cardiovascular and metabolic health.

These may include blood pressure, cholesterol, blood glucose, and other measurements recommended by your healthcare professional.

A single number rarely tells the entire story.

Results should be interpreted within the larger context of your health history, medications, other test results, and individual risk factors.

KNOW YOUR NUMBERS

Do not just collect test results. Ask what they mean, whether they have changed, and whether you need to do anything next.

Keep vaccinations on your preventive-care list

Vaccination is another important part of preventive health.

Recommended vaccines can change throughout adulthood based on age, health conditions, pregnancy, occupation, travel, previous vaccination history, and other factors.

Reviewing your vaccination history periodically with a healthcare professional can help identify whether you are due for anything.

Know your family health history

Family health history can provide important information about your risk for certain conditions.

If possible, learn about major health conditions that have affected close biological relatives, including the approximate age when those conditions developed.

This information can help healthcare professionals determine whether some screening or prevention recommendations should be adjusted.

Keep an accurate medication list

Knowing what medications and supplements you take is an important part of routine healthcare.

Keep a current list that includes prescription medications, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, and supplements.

Include the dose when you know it.

Bring that list to healthcare visits and update it whenever something changes.

Remember that prevention happens between appointments

Preventive health is not limited to screenings and medical visits.

Everyday behaviors also contribute to long-term health.

Regular physical activity, nutritious eating patterns, adequate sleep, avoiding tobacco, limiting harmful substance use, managing health conditions, and maintaining social connection can all play a role.

You do not need to optimize every behavior at once. Consistent, realistic improvements can be more useful than short periods of perfection.

Keep your own basic health record

Healthcare can become difficult to track, especially when you see more than one provider.

Keeping a simple personal record can make it easier to remember important information.

Consider keeping track of

  • Current medications and supplements
  • Major diagnoses and procedures
  • Vaccination history
  • Recent screening dates and results
  • Allergies
  • Family health history
  • Questions for your next healthcare visit

Make your appointments more useful

Healthcare visits can move quickly. A little preparation can help you use the time well.

Before your appointment, write down your most important questions or concerns.

If you receive a new diagnosis, test result, medication, or recommendation, ask what it means and what you should do next.

It is reasonable to ask for clarification when something is unclear.

YOUR SIMPLE ACTION PLAN

Take one preventive-health step.

You do not need to organize your entire medical history today. Start with one useful action.

01

Update your medication list.

02

Check when you last had routine care.

03

Write down your family health history.

04

Ask whether you are due for screenings or vaccines.
